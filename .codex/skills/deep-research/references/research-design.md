# Research Design

Use this reference to decide what evidence is needed before collecting it. The goal is to answer a decision-relevant question with the smallest reliable research effort, not to run a generic study.

## Question Framing

State the decision, the uncertainty, the affected audience or system, and what evidence would change the decision. Distinguish a discovery question (what problem or behavior exists), an evaluative question (whether a design or flow works), and a measurement question (how often or how much).

## Method Selection

| Question | Prefer | Avoid treating as proof |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What the current repository does | Code, tests, runtime observations, requirements | A stale document or assumption |
| Why users behave as they do | Past-behavior interviews, support cases, usability observations | Hypothetical preference questions |
| How frequent a behavior is | Analytics, surveys, support volumes | A handful of qualitative anecdotes |
| Whether an interface is understandable | Representative-task usability testing | General opinions about the idea |
| Whether a standard or market changed | Dated primary documentation or authoritative source | Undated summaries or copied claims |

For interviews, ask for concrete prior behavior: trigger, actions, alternatives, constraints, and outcome. Use neutral, one-part questions. Do not infer population-level frequency from a small qualitative sample.

## Evidence Record

For each material item, record source, date/version, direct observation or quote, interpretation, affected decision, and limitations. Attribute direct quotes verbatim; label paraphrases as paraphrases. Do not make the evidence stronger than its provenance supports.

## Bias And Quality Checks

- Seek disconfirming evidence for important hypotheses.
- Separate observed behavior from stated preference.
- Check whether the source population, time period, and environment match the decision.
- Triangulate high-impact conclusions with an independent evidence type when feasible.
- Treat a missing baseline, target, or data source as an explicit gap rather than inventing one.
